Automatic text summarization aims to produce concise and coherent summaries that preserve the essential information from source documents. Extractive methods select salient sentences from the original text using statistical features, graph-based ranking, or neural scoring functions, ensuring grammatical correctness but often producing redundant and disjointed outputs. Abstractive approaches generate novel text using encoder-decoder architectures with attention mechanisms, enabling paraphrasing and information fusion that more closely resembles human-written summaries. Pre-trained sequence-to-sequence models such as BART and T5 have established new performance standards on benchmark datasets including CNN/DailyMail and XSum by leveraging large-scale pre-training objectives. Multi-document summarization extends the single-document setting by synthesizing information from multiple related sources, requiring cross-document coreference resolution and redundancy elimination. Faithfulness and factual consistency represent critical challenges, as abstractive models may hallucinate content not supported by the source documents. Evaluation metrics such as ROUGE measure lexical overlap with reference summaries but correlate imperfectly with human judgments of summary quality. Recent work explores reference-free evaluation using question answering and natural language inference to assess factual consistency without gold-standard summaries. Controllable summarization allows users to specify desired attributes such as length, style, and topic focus, increasing the practical utility of summarization systems.
